The Salt Road Confession
by Corvin Ashgrove

The caravan had been three days out of Auldwyn when Master Hale finally
asked the question everyone had been too polite to raise.

"You're not a merchant," he said, not unkindly, over the low fire. "A
merchant doesn't flinch every time a crow lands on the wagon rail."

Kestrel didn't answer right away. The salt road stretched pale and cracked
ahead of them, catching the last grey light like a wound that wouldn't
close. Somewhere behind, three days behind, was the reason for the
flinching.

"I was a debt collector," she said finally. "For the wrong house."

Hale said nothing. That was the thing about the salt road -- it collected
confessions the way the flats collected the tide, indifferent and total.
By the time they reached the crossing at Ninth Well, Kestrel had told him
about the ledger, about the girl in Lowmarket who couldn't pay, about the
choice she'd made instead of the one she'd been paid to make.

"They'll have riders out," Hale said.

"They already do."

He looked at her for a long moment, then at the crows circling low over
the wagon, unusually many of them, unusually patient.

"Well," he said, banking the fire down to embers. "Best you learn to drive
a team properly, then. A woman who can't tell a crow from a courier is no
use to me on watch."

It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even trust, not yet. But it was room on
the wagon, and on the salt road, in the flat grey country between one life
and the next, that was as close to mercy as anyone was likely to get.
